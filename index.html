02 / Taxonomy

A name, four centuries in the making

First described as the “baleine de la Nouvelle Angleterre” by Brisson in 1756, formalised by Borowski in 1781, placed in the genus Megaptera by Gray in 1846 and restored to Borowski’s epithet by Kellogg in 1932.

The common name comes from the arching of the back as the whale dives. Historical synonyms include Balaena gibbosa, B. boops, B. nodosa, B. longimana and Megaptera versabilis.

03 / Anatomy

Built for long distance

  1. Pectoral finsUp to about 5 m — roughly a third of body length; the longest appendages of any animal.
  2. TuberclesKnobbly bumps on head and jaw, each holding a single hair thought to act as a sensor.
  3. BodyDark dorsal surface, pale pleated throat that expands during lunge feeding; small dorsal fin on a hump.
  4. BaleenPlates that work as a sieve, straining prey from engulfed water.
  5. Fluke fingerprintUnique pigment, scars, barnacle marks and edge notches, stable for life — the basis of photo-identification.
  6. EpibiontsBodies host barnacles and whale lice.

Photo-ID in practice

Researchers photograph the fluke as the whale dives and match it to catalogues. Happywhale combines AI image recognition with human review to match photos from scientists, tour operators and the public — tracking individuals across oceans and decades.

298

Distinct humpbacks identified in a single day off South Africa’s west coast (Dec 2025) — a world record for large whales.

395

Individuals identified across the three-day stretch, 29–31 December 2025.

20+

Whales within five body lengths: a “super-group”, essentially unknown to science before 2011.

04 / Distribution & migration

Five thousand miles, on an empty stomach

Humpbacks live in every ocean and make one of the longest migrations of any mammal. Most fast for months during migration and breeding, living on blubber stored during the feeding season.

Spotlight · Relevant to India

The Arabian Sea humpback

The world’s smallest, most isolated and most endangered humpback population — genetically separated from other Indian Ocean humpbacks for roughly 70,000 years and possibly a distinct subspecies.

Illegal Soviet whaling killed at least 242 animals off Oman, Pakistan and India in the 1960s. Photo-ID in 2000–2004 suggested fewer than 100 remained off Oman, and 30–40% of identified whales there show entanglement scars. Satellite tracking shows some whales move between Oman and India.

05 / Diet & feeding

Nets made of air

Humpbacks engulf vast volumes of water and strain krill and small fish through their baleen. In the south, Antarctic krill depend on sea ice — tying whale health directly to the ice.

  1. Bubble-net feedingWhales spiral beneath prey releasing air; the rising cylinder of bubbles concentrates the school before they lunge up through the centre.
  2. Cooperative vs soloA January 2026 Proceedings B study of Canadian Pacific humpbacks points to social learning in how the technique spreads.
  3. Super-groupsOff South Africa, aggregations of 20+ whales are linked to Benguela-current upwelling.
  4. Acoustic roleModelling suggests calls directed into bubble nets may help herd prey.
06 / Song & culture

4–33 minutesminutes per song

A song that travels between oceans

Male humpbacks produce complex, structured songs that carry far underwater. All males in a population sing roughly the same song, which changes progressively through a season.

New song types spread between populations across ocean basins — a striking example of cultural transmission in a non-human animal. Converging migratory routes help songs pass from one population to the next.

07 / Reproduction

Born in warm, shallow water

  1. Mating systemPolygynandrous — both sexes have multiple partners; males follow females and compete in “competitive groups”.
  2. CalvingCalves are born on tropical breeding grounds and nurse on milk made from the mother’s stored fat.
  3. Body conditionThin mothers may not produce enough milk — food shortages show up as low calf counts.
  4. Maternal attachmentIn Sept 2026, Griffith University researchers reported a Gold Coast mother staying with her presumed stillborn calf for hours, possibly days.
08 / Conservation status

A recovery, not yet a rescue

Before the 1985 whaling moratorium most populations had fallen by more than 95%. The species is now increasing across much of its range — though several populations remain small or are declining again under climate pressure.

09 / Threats

What stands in the way

  • Entanglement

    Fishing gear and debris — the leading human cause of death. Whales can drag gear for months and thousands of miles.

10 / Latest updates

2026, in the field

  1. 19 Nov 2026

    Southern Hemisphere calving crash

    Off Ningaloo, daily calf sightings fell from a 12-year average of ~10 to fewer than 2; Moreton Bay calves were down ~90% on 2025. Low counts also came from Ecuador, Brazil, Réunion and New Caledonia — linked to Antarctic sea-ice loss.

  2. 19 Nov 2026

    Hawai‘i decline

    Breeding numbers peaked at just over 15,000 in 2008–09 and fell to ~9,900 by 2022–23, with the sharpest drop during the 2014–16 “Blob” heatwave.

  3. 17 Nov 2026

    Genomes from whale blow

    St Andrews and partners sequenced whole humpback genomes from drone-collected breath — a world first; 58 samples from 26 whales matched biopsy results.

  4. 15–18 Nov 2026

    Oregon stranding

    A young humpback entangled in crab gear stranded near Yachats; the Confederated Tribes of Siletz Indians salvaged it, reviving a traditional practice.

  5. 12 Nov 2026

    Heatwave physiology

    A University of Hawai‘i study used baleen records to show how marine heatwaves disrupt metabolism and reproduction — with concern for the 2026–27 El Niño.

  6. 12 Nov 2026

    Hudson River sighting

    A humpback in New York’s Hudson River; sightings near the city are increasingly common.

  7. 10 Nov 2026

    Western Australia

    Observable migrating numbers down ~60%, with fewer calves, more strandings and thin mothers.

  8. Jun 2026

    Arabian Sea telemetry

    Satellite-tracking study of the endangered Arabian Sea population published in Frontiers in Marine Science.

12 / Best practices

Watch well

Distance is the single most important thing you control.

  • Keep at least 100 yards (~91 m) away unless stricter local rules apply.
  • In Hawai‘i and Alaska it is illegal to approach within 100 yards; in Hawai‘i aircraft and drones must stay 1,000 ft away.
  • Never intercept, chase, swim with, touch or try to elicit a reaction.
  • Back away at signs of disturbance — sudden speed changes, prolonged dives, tail-swishing to shield a calf.
  • Choose operators that follow official guidelines (e.g., NOAA Whale SENSE, Be Whale Wise).

  • Slow down and post a lookout in whale areas — “See a spout… watch out”.
  • Report sightings via apps such as Whale Alert to help reduce ship strikes.

  • Do not attempt to disentangle it yourself — only authorised, trained responders may do so.
  • Report immediately to the local stranding/entanglement hotline, keep a safe distance and, if possible, stand by and photograph.

  • Prefer non-invasive methods: photo-ID, drones, acoustic monitoring, blow sampling.
  • Target the most upwind whale, avoid resting animals, and stop if a whale reacts.

  • Reduce plastic use and prevent fishing-gear loss; support slow-speed shipping zones and whale-safe fisheries.
